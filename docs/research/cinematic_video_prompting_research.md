# Cinematic camera, story, and style guidance for video prompts

## Acceptance scenarios

User wants a short cinematic video. Given the same brief, source facts, cast, duration, and selected style, When MediaOverload prepares its story and video description, Then each camera choice has a visible narrative purpose and physically coherent movement, the requested visual style remains consistent across the shots, and a requested causal story advances to a readable change or payoff without adding unsupported facts.

User wants a cute, lively character video that feels pleasant to keep watching. Given a character-led brief and a selected style, When MediaOverload prepares the opening image and moving-video prompt, Then the character has a readable goal, expressive anticipation, a distinctive physical action, and a satisfying reaction or payoff; the camera helps the viewer follow that action, and a small amount of character- and style-appropriate secondary motion keeps the scene alive without burying the main action. An explicitly still or serious brief keeps its requested tone, and a news-grounded scene adds no unsupported event or joke.

## Findings from the references

### Melies: name the operation and its job

The Cinematic Techniques page organizes 424 entries across camera movement, framing, angles, lighting, composition, optics, color, time, effects, editing, atmosphere, and genre looks. Its useful unit is not a technique label alone: the linked entries explain the camera operation, its narrative use, a neighboring technique it is often confused with, and concrete prompt wording.

The camera entries make the distinction concrete: a pan rotates from one planted position and should connect two facts in the same geography; a dolly physically translates and reveals parallax; a zoom changes focal length from a fixed position; a tracking shot travels with the subject and matches its pace. Melies calls constant unmotivated drift a weak choice and asks a prompt to name the movement, the spatial relationship, and the landing frame. Those mechanics give a video model a more observable instruction than “cinematic camera movement.”

Sources: [Melies Cinematic Techniques](https://melies.co/cinematic-techniques); [Pan](https://melies.co/cinematic-techniques/camera-movement/pan); [Dolly In](https://melies.co/cinematic-techniques/camera-movement/dolly-in); [Tracking Shot](https://melies.co/cinematic-techniques/camera-movement/tracking).

### Opuscar: separate story direction from the style specification

Lemo-Opuscar separates a director guide, a technical guide, and one style file per visual style. Its director guide starts with a one-subject/one-goal/one-turn story, then asks for a logline and arc, a shot list whose framing, angle, camera move, duration, and reason are explicit, and one medium-native visual move at the emotional peak. The style files describe the medium's construction, color logic, motion, camera grammar, native transitions, and pitfalls. The watercolor and ink-wash examples also show that two superficially related painted looks need different rules for marks, color, motion, and transitions.

This is a useful prompt organization pattern. The repository says the demonstrations were made frame-by-frame in code, with no video-generation model, and that the style prompts were tuned for Claude Opus 5.5. It does not establish that its prompts or sample films improve MediaOverload's video providers.

Sources: [Lemo-Opuscar repository](https://github.com/lemomo-ai/lemo-opuscar), [DIRECTOR.md](https://github.com/lemomo-ai/lemo-opuscar/blob/main/DIRECTOR.md), [TECHNIQUE.md](https://github.com/lemomo-ai/lemo-opuscar/blob/main/TECHNIQUE.md), [Watercolor style specification](https://github.com/lemomo-ai/lemo-opuscar/blob/main/styles/watercolor/STYLE.md), [Ink-wash style specification](https://github.com/lemomo-ai/lemo-opuscar/blob/main/styles/ink-wash/STYLE.md).

### Character appeal: readable performance, not nonstop camera activity

The Opuscar director guide calls for anticipation, action, and follow-through, and for characters to change emotion rather than slide across the frame. Its style files show why motion belongs partly to the character and partly to the medium: watercolor paints forms in and uses restrained sway; ink wash lets strokes define movement and contrasts brief stillness with a decisive stroke; the halftone cartoon gives characters blinks and elastic landings and gives impactful graphic events a timed overshoot. The director guide also calls for varied pace, including an acceleration or deceleration and a breath, so the viewer can read a gag or reaction.

For MediaOverload, that suggests a short performance arc: show what the character wants, load a recognizable pose or facial cue, let a physical action alter the scene, then give the character a brief, legible response. Let a blink, cheek puff, bounce, recoil, accessory lag, or nearby object react only when it fits that character and selected medium. The action should stay visible through most of the clip, while its speed and scale change around a payoff; continuous camera motion by itself is not character performance.

Sources: [DIRECTOR.md, sections 3–9](https://github.com/lemomo-ai/lemo-opuscar/blob/main/DIRECTOR.md), [Watercolor style specification, sections 5–6](https://github.com/lemomo-ai/lemo-opuscar/blob/main/styles/watercolor/STYLE.md), [Chinese Ink Wash style specification, sections 5–6](https://github.com/lemomo-ai/lemo-opuscar/blob/main/styles/ink-wash/STYLE.md), [Halftone Dossier style specification, sections 2 and 5–6](https://github.com/lemomo-ai/lemo-opuscar/blob/main/styles/halftone-dossier/STYLE.md).

## Application to MediaOverload

MediaOverload already has news-specific causal and factual-boundary contracts, per-shot action/cause/state/camera fields, and a story-specific `world.visual_language`. The first local patch made visible action and camera movement the default, but the shared prompt did not yet specify character performance or which details should make a style visually consistent. The updated contract fills that gap while retaining the existing source boundaries and story fields.

A read-only trace of a live long-video run exposed two gaps between the story and the renderer. The story call returned a broad `visual` description, while its later normalizer kept only `segment_id`, `visual`, and `narration`; even when action and camera fields were available, segment-prompt preparation did not carry them into the render prompt. Also, the brief had already produced an `opening_keyframe_prompt`, but the first long-video anchor resolved only `prompt`. The result was a still atmospheric opener, and a later beat mixed tracking with panning without stating either move's distinct operation. The revised segment contract now asks for and retains action, physical cause, visible result, and camera direction, then carries those details into the final segment prompt. The first anchor now receives the frozen opening-frame prompt plus the first action as an anticipation cue. Shared illustration direction preserves composition and readable performance while matching the selected medium instead of adding watercolor to every style. The in-flight run was created before these changes and is not evidence for them.

The first local prompt patch added an action-first contract and a separate still opening-frame staging direction. This follow-up adds the character-performance cues and clarifies camera support, pacing, and the visible style rules to carry across shots. These are prompt hypotheses; source descriptions and unit checks cannot prove an audience response or a rendered quality gain.

The current creative direction should keep three layers distinct:

- **Story cause:** what the character wants, what physically changes, and why the next beat follows. News facts remain attached to their real actor, place, and consequence; fictional character behavior is a visual response, not a replacement event.
- **Camera grammar:** opening framing, one coherent movement or intentional lock, the space it travels through or reveals, why it supports this beat, and the ending composition. Do not hand the model a bag of unrelated camera terms.
- **Style and performance:** stable surface, palette/light, and transition rules; character-specific facial/body performance; and only those secondary motions that fit the character and medium.

The matched-render treatment and control use the same user brief, style, model, seed, and rendering controls:

- Camera descriptions name framing, one motivated camera operation (or a locked frame), its direction and spatial relationship to the subject, its narrative purpose, and the resulting view. Pan/tilt, physical travel, follow/tracking, and optical zoom retain distinct mechanics. The camera keeps the character's face, goal, and path readable rather than calling attention to itself.
- Style is expressed with a coherent medium, surface or mark quality, palette/light, and motion/transition behavior, held across shots. Character motion can be lively while a watercolor, paper-cut, or other medium remains visually consistent. A medium-native reveal is used only when it helps the story turn.
- Character performance shows anticipation, physical action with follow-through, and a short reaction. A brief blink, bounce, squash/recoil, gaze shift, or accessory/environment response is chosen only if supported by the character and style. Pace varies around the payoff; static holds are brief and purposeful.
- Non-news Native H3 stories use one goal, one complication that changes the next action, and one visible payoff that resolves or reframes the opening. Single-action requests remain single-action requests.
- Source-fact alignment remains a scored guardrail; no automatic creative review gate is added.

The existing render matrix is ten distinct story briefs with two matched seeds each (20 A/B pairs, 40 clips). It records the resolved model/configuration, seed, prompt lineage, videos, technical QA and contact sheets. A clip counts as technically usable only when the workflow QA passes, the pinned text/vision model is confirmed, and ffprobe reports a finite positive duration plus a video stream with nonzero dimensions. A blind X/Y gallery and score sheet cover brief/source fidelity, story progression, camera purpose/mechanics, character performance/charm, motion pacing, style consistency, identity/geography continuity, opening/payoff fidelity, and overall visual quality. The scorecard distinguishes performance and pacing from camera activity. After the five-case matched review below favored the directing package, the shared prompt now includes its strongest story-arc, style-continuity, and framing-margin rules. This is a limited promotion from one seed per brief and one reviewer; likes, retention, and broad audience preference remain unmeasured.

## Evidence limits

The two references teach filmmaking and style specification; neither measures likes, mood, or audience retention. The working assumption that lively, appealing character performance helps people stay with the video must be treated as a creative hypothesis, not an established engagement result. The sources support clearer prompt structure and more precise camera language, not a measured claim that generated video quality improves. Only same-model matched renders and frame/video review can assess visible gains. Even a small blind review cannot establish audience preference or broad superiority from its prompt set.

### Camera-scale refinement from the pilot

The prompt/contact-sheet review exposed a specific gap: asking for a tilt while also changing the character's apparent size does not say how that scale change happens. The shared video contract now says a pan or tilt changes the camera's direction, not distance or subject scale; a scale change must follow from camera translation, optical zoom, or subject travel. It also asks for one continuous camera operation per shot, an ending frame that follows from that operation, and a payoff frame that keeps the character's reaction and story-critical result legible together. A reaction close-up follows a clearly shown result. These rules target an observed prompt inconsistency; the new wording still needs matched renders before claiming a visual improvement.
